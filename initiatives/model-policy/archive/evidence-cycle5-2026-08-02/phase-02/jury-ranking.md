# Ranking — juror

- ranked: item-5 > item-4 > item-1 > item-2 > item-3 > item-6
- reference-position: unset

## Why item-5 over item-4
- item-5 shows every category — hearings, documents, tasks, notes, and the full caseload of
  other matters — simultaneously, so "what has happened and what's due" needs no scroll or
  click; item-4's ledger requires moving past the compose row and month groups to reach the
  same documents and notes.
- item-5 states the overdue count's composition inline ("OVERDUE 2 · 1 task · 1 document")
  where item-4's meta table gives only the bare number "2" — the former answers "overdue on
  what" without a second look.
- item-5 supplies a case-to-case switcher plus a full row-level keyboard system (J/K move,
  N next case with overdue) that directly answers the brief's named gap — no way yet to move
  from "done with this case" to the next one that needs him. item-4's keyboard legend covers
  filters only, with no path to another case.
- This is close: item-4's typography and its "what the case still owes" framing are excellent,
  and its reference block is the most economically labelled of the six. But item-5 does
  everything item-4 does and adds the multi-case view without losing any of that clarity.

## Why item-4 over item-1
- item-4 opens with an explicit "What the case still owes" block before the compose row,
  naming the overdue task, the overdue document and the unset next hearing together in one
  place; item-1 carries the same facts but folds them into the top of a long ledger, one step
  further from the eye.
- item-4 runs its case metadata in two columns and uses the available canvas for actual case
  facts; item-1 stacks the identical fields into one narrow column, leaving roughly half the
  page as plain margin either side.
- item-1's footer mixes real case identity ("Iyer & Ananth, Advocates · O.S. No. 412 of 2024 ·
  30 Jul 2026") with unmarked prose describing the layout's own responsive behaviour ("Below
  46rem the record stays one column…") — that sentence explains the design rather than being
  the design, and it sits outside the block already labelled as reference, so a reader meets
  it as though it were case content. item-4's equivalent material sits behind an explicit
  label — "State reference — for design review, not part of the case record" — before any of
  it appears.

## Why item-1 over item-2
- item-1 renders every historical entry — hearings, documents, notes, month groups — cleanly,
  with an inline, in-place error state ("Not saved — the connection dropped…") that reads as
  the record's actual current condition rather than a staged demo.
- item-2's Documents card breaks under its own content: the word "JUL" wraps mid-word across
  two lines inside the Affidavit row, and the FILE/EDIT/REMOVE controls crowd against the
  due-date text in the same cramped box — a genuine collision, not a stylistic choice.
- item-2 also leaves a wide band of empty background between the five-card row and the
  reference footer, reading as an unfinished middle of the page rather than an intentional
  pause.
- item-2's caseload switcher and full-width card grid are a real, direct answer to the brief's
  named width debt and its missing next-case flow — the strongest thing it does — but the
  visible defects sitting inside its primary content area cost it more than that gains it.

## Why item-2 over item-3
- item-2 still shows the actual hearing, document, task and note rows for the case — the real
  history the brief's job-in-one-sentence asks for — despite its layout problems.
- item-3 shows almost none of that: the page is one active step plus two blocked-next-step
  lines; the case's hearing history, document list and notes exist only as a bare count
  ("3 · 3 · 2 · 2") in a small footer strip, never as visible rows.
- item-3's stepper concept is well executed on its own terms — no collision, and its "End of
  run" idea for handing off to the next case is a thoughtful, explicit answer to the brief's
  named expert-path gap — but as submitted the page cannot tell a lawyer what has already
  happened on the case, only what is due right now.
- item-3's reference block is the most fully written of the six (Empty / Disabled / Error and
  success / Keys / Reflow / One case, each explained in a full sentence), which is real craft,
  but it also occupies roughly a third of the total page.

## Why item-3 over item-6
- item-3's step rail renders three clean, separated labels. item-6's equivalent rail runs the
  three step names together with no space or separator — "RECORD HEARING OUTCOMEADD
  HEARINGFILE DOCUMENT" — a plainly broken piece of text sitting directly under the page's
  primary navigation.
- item-3's step 3 states the real, current fact about the affidavit ("due 24 Jul 2026 · not
  filed · Overdue"); item-6's step 3 instead claims filing is blocked until the next hearing
  is added — a dependency that does not match the case's actual overdue document, so the page
  asserts something the record does not support.
- item-3's reference section is fully written; item-6's stops at a single bare label
  ("REFERENCE — LOADING STATE") with no content shown beneath it and a stretch of empty page
  below it — reading as an unfinished screen next to item-3's completed one.

## What would change my mind
item-3's "End of run" idea — a designed moment where finishing a case's open step hands the
lawyer to the next case that needs him, explained in its own reference notes — is a more
literal, more designed answer to the brief's "what does not exist yet" expert-path question
than anything in item-5. item-5 gives a caseload switcher and a keyboard shortcut to jump
there, which is real and useful, but it is a list, not a closing act. If item-3 had paired that
handoff concept with an actual on-page record of what happened on the case, it would have been
the strongest thing in the set.
